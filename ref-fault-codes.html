Fault Code Log — Real, Ghost & Derived

2009 DB9 Coupé · 5.9L V12First-hand diagnosis · April 2026← All reference topics

From the misfire diagnosis on this car. Much of it isn't in the official workshop manual. The full story is in the misfire diagnostic report.

This page explains how to read the misfire codes on a 2009 Aston Martin DB9 V12: what P0300, P0301 to P0312 and P1656 mean, which ECU and bank each one points to, and how to tell a real misfire from a ghost one. The worked example is the April 2026 blocked-injector misfire on this car.

What the misfire codes mean

CodeMeaningWhere it points
P0300Random or multiple misfires detectedOften a consequence of a single-cylinder code, as in the April 2026 case. On its own, with no cylinder-specific code, think fuel pressure, air metering (MAF), the ignition system or a vacuum leak
P0301 to P0306Misfire on cylinder 1 to 6Driver-side bank, primary ECU (EMS-P)
P0307 to P0312Misfire on cylinder 7 to 12Passenger-side bank, secondary ECU (EMS-S)
P1656Misfire on startup (first 1,000 revolutions)Seen as a derived code in the April 2026 case
P1000System check not completed since memory clearExpected after clearing codes. It clears itself after a full drive cycle

The cylinder number in a code is the global 1 to 12 number. The V12 is run as two six-cylinder engines, and each ECU also numbers its own cylinders 1 to 6, so live data on a scanner can disagree with the code. See V12 cylinder numbering and twin-ECU mapping and the ThinkDiag numbering caveats.

Which cause to suspect for a single-cylinder code

A code for one cylinder usually comes down to the coil pack, the spark plug or the injector. After ultrasonic injector cleaning, a debris-clogged injector is the classic sequel, and that is what happened on this car. The ultrasonic injector cleaning protocol covers the flushing step that prevents it, and the dead-cylinder checklist lists what to rule out before a three-hour stripdown. If you have just changed plugs or coil packs, the misfire correction relearn is the next step.

Real, ghost and derived codes

The practical rule: a single confirmed dead cylinder on one bank of this V12 can generate 1 to 2 ghost misfire codes on adjacent or cross-bank cylinders. Identify and fix the true dead cylinder first, and the sympathy codes clear on their own.

These are the codes that were present during the April 2026 misfire diagnosis:

CodeDescriptionVerdictResolution
P0307 Cylinder 7 misfire detected Real EMS-S Inj 1 blocked — injector cleaned and refitted
P0305 Cylinder 5 misfire detected Ghost Sympathy/compensation code — cleared when Cyl 7 restored
P0300 Random/multiple misfire Derived Consequence of P0307 — cleared automatically
P1656 Misfire on startup (first 1,000 rev) Derived Cleared automatically
P1000 System check not completed since memory clear Expected Self-clears after full drive cycle (~few days)

Why a ghost misfire code appears

The VH V12 uses a Ford-derived neural network misfire detection system (JPL/NASA-origin technology, first production use on the DB9 in 2004), inferring misfires from crankshaft deceleration pulses. A consistently dead cylinder creates a large rotational irregularity that can be misread as a misfire on an adjacent firing-order position. On top of that, unburnt fuel and excess oxygen pumped through the dead cylinder corrupts the O2 sensor readings for the bank — the ECU leans the whole bank in compensation, which can push borderline cylinders into misfiring at idle. Some ECUs also retard timing across the bank to protect the catalysts, which can itself tip a marginal cylinder over.

Check the battery before chasing codes

VH-platform Astons are very sensitive to low battery voltage. Modules start going offline or logging electrical faults at around 11.8 V or below, and most don't shut down gracefully: they fault and latch. A car that has sat, or has an ageing battery, can show a pile of unrelated codes across several modules.

Measure the battery at rest, with the ignition off and the battery not recently charged. A healthy AGM battery reads 12.6 to 12.8 V, and 12.2 to 12.5 V is borderline. Below 12.2 V, charge it fully and retest before interpreting any code. Then clear the codes, run the car for five minutes or more and rescan. Codes that don't return were ghosts, and codes that return are real.

A sensible order of work

  1. Check the battery voltage, as above
  2. Scan every module, not just the engine ECUs, and read both engine ECUs. The car has two diagnostic ports: both engine ECUs show on the powertrain port, everything else on the body port, so scan both
  3. Record every code before clearing anything, with a screenshot or a saved report
  4. Find the true dead cylinder first (see the dead-cylinder checklist above); the sympathy codes will clear
  5. Fix it, clear the codes, take a short drive and rescan. Returning codes are real
  6. After plugs or coil packs, do the misfire correction relearn
