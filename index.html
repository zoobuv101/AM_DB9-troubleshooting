October 2026

Differential oil change

Done ✓

Graziano AM803D rear diff drained and refilled at 64,098 miles — very likely the first change in 17 years. 1.4 L came out; the strainer was clean and the drain-plug magnet carried only a light film of fine metallic paste, consistent with age and mileage — no chips or flakes. Refilled with just under 1.5 L to a slow drip from the fill hole. Strainer bolts 5 Nm; drain (14 mm hex) and fill (22 mm) plugs 49 Nm.

Why this oil. The factory fill is Shell Spirax S6 AXME/AXRME 75W-90 (API GL-5, MT-1), which is near-unobtainable in the UK. The non-negotiable is API GL-5: the AM803D is a hypoid crown wheel and pinion behind a 6.0 V12, and GL-5 carries roughly twice the extreme-pressure additive of GL-4. That ruled out the GL-4 / “GL4+” manual-gearbox oils (Castrol Transmax Manual Multivehicle, Liqui Moly 4434) despite their 75W-90 rating. Comma SX75W-90 GL-5 meets GL-5, sits very close to the factory oil on viscosity (104 / 16.5 cSt at 40 / 100 °C vs Shell’s 103 / 15), and was on the shelf at Halfords. It isn’t LS-rated, so it went in pre-mixed with Westway LSD friction modifier at 40 ml per litre — in line with Aston dealer practice, which adds LSD additive even to the factory oil.

Next time. The Comma is semi-synthetic and doesn’t carry MT-1, so this is a sound interim fill rather than the long-term answer. We’ll revisit in 12 months (October 2027) and will probably move to Motul Gear 300 LS 75W-90 — fully synthetic, GL-5, with the LS additive built in.

February 2026

Gearbox service

Done ✓

A full ZF 6HP26 Touchtronic II service, despite Aston's "filled for life" claim (ZF itself recommends a fluid change from 80,000 km depending on use). I used the genuine ZF service kit from Gearbox Oils Ltd: the combined sump/filter, mechatronic connector sleeve, valve-body transfer tubes, the fill adapter and 10 litres of genuine ZF fluid.

The job was done in a friend's double garage, with the car at full height on the QuickJack, which has probably paid for itself six times over by now. I followed the proper procedure throughout, including using my airline to blow the old fluid out of the remote oil cooler. The sump/filter, the connector sleeve and the valve-body tubes were all replaced. The box was then refilled to the correct level using ZF's official method: engine running and selector in P, with the fluid topped up through the filler until it reaches the level of the fill hole at the specified temperature. I read the temperature live from the gearbox on ThinkDiag.

A fairly straightforward job, and fascinating to see the gearbox internals, especially with the valve body out.

August 2025

Oil & filter change

Done ✓

Wow, I'd forgotten what a pain this is compared with my other cars! The filter was the OEM part from HWM, and the oil was Mobil 1 FS 0W-40, the grade Aston Martin recommends in the owner's handbook, from the 20-litre drum I'd bought for the previous change.

The filter is reached from the top, but only after removing the right-hand air inlet from the manifold. There's no easy way to stop the oil in the filter dropping down over the front subframe, so it's a messy job with some cleaning up afterwards. There's no easy access to the drain plug either: the whole front undertray has to come off, held on by what feels like a million bolts!

July 2025

PPF fitted

Done ✓

After plenty of sunshine, the fresh paint and clear coat had fully hardened, so it was time for paint protection film. I used a pre-cut Aston Martin kit from Aliath, and we fitted it at another friend's house because he has a double garage.

There's something oddly satisfying about soaking a huge "sticker" and squeegeeing the water out until it sits perfectly. It's not an easy job to get the film on and positioned exactly right, but the kit fitted very accurately. It's protected the front end ever since: a great result, with all the vulnerable areas now fully covered.

June 2025

Dash trim & facelift wiper arms

Done ✓

Like most of these cars, the sun eventually got the better of the leather on top of the dashboard, especially on the passenger side. At first I softened and stretched the leather and stuck it back down. That sort of worked for a while, but I was always on the lookout for a replacement or a re-trim. I even considered doing both sides in black Alcantara, which wouldn't have looked out of place next to the contrasting light Alcantara headlining and A-pillars.

Then I found a UK-spec, right-hand-drive DB9 being broken in Holland, with the same interior colour and a few other useful bits. The dash trim went straight in the basket. While browsing the rest, I spotted the facelift wiper arms, something I'd looked at before. They're fully OEM, but take the modern blade-style wipers, and unlike anything I'd seen on eBay the price was sensible. So I ordered those too.

Thanks to Brexit, of course, our own government wanted more of my money to import the parts from Holland. That's even more interesting given it was a UK car that had obviously met its fate over there... go figure! A few days later, and after applying for an import licence number, the parts arrived and went on. The interior is back to pristine, and the car now has facelift wiper arms with blade wipers.

August 2024

Fuel cap release solenoid & cable

Done ✓

The fuel filler flap refused to open at the petrol station, so an investigation was needed. The release solenoid is mounted in the rear wing and can be reached from the boot, and that's also how you release the flap by hand: you move the solenoid manually. Luckily that was enough to get the flap open and fuel the car, but it clearly needed sorting.

With the solenoid out, I could see its white plastic cable mount had gone brittle with age and broken. I assumed that was the fault and cable-tied it back in position. The solenoid worked and the cable moved, but something still wasn't right. Following the cable back to the release catch, which is built into the cable, I found the real problem. Water had got into the catch and corroded the spring, which had snapped, so it was working on only about 70% of the spring. I considered rebuilding the cable with a new spring, but HWM's price for a replacement was reasonable, so a new cable went in.

Research on the solenoid turned up an identical unit used by both BMW and Jaguar. It's a VDO part (label 2W93-F3C070-AB, a Jaguar part number), and it's the white plastic cable mount that's specific to certain cars, which is how the BMW and Jaguar matches came to light. A replacement from eBay later, the fuel flap is fully working again. The Ford-era parts bin strikes again.

June 2023

Secondary decat

Done ✓

Secondary cats out, polished stainless pipes in. The V12 finally clears its throat.

The brief: more of the V12's voice, without the boom and drone of the Mansory rear box, which didn't last long on the car. After some research, a secondary decat ticked every box. The primary cats and rear silencers stay standard, so the note opens up without becoming antisocial.

The pipes are AMS Edition replacement secondary straight pipes in polished stainless (AMS Edition, Isle of Wight). They're a straight bolt-in replacement for the secondary cats, with no remap needed. Interestingly, Aston Martin offered the same idea as an option, with the pipes supplied by QuickSilver.

Excellent quality, and exactly the sound I was after: quiet around town, and properly glorious but still classy when you open her up. It's how the car should have left the factory.

April 2023

Discs & pads all round

Done ✓

Done while the car was at the paint shop for its respray, with the wheels off and everything easy to reach. The discs came from a donor car that had covered very few miles. Their faces were like new, with no visible wear or lip, so they went straight on.

Pads: Portfield R4-S on all four corners. It's an uprated fast-road compound: noticeably stronger bite and better pedal feel than the standard pads, with much less dust, so the wheels stay clean. The separate handbrake got standard Brembo pads.

Paste: the pad backs, pins and caliper contact points got the correct Aston-spec Tunap MP 113 (AM part 705065), applied as Service Bulletin SB-06-0344V2 (Recommended Brake Maintenance Procedures) describes. That means every surface where the pad touches the caliper, the ends of the backplate and the pin holes. Not a single squeal since. The R4-S pads have been excellent: strong, consistent stopping, and they stay quiet and clean.

April 2023

Full respray

Done ✓

The big one. A few areas were showing the inevitable aluminium corrosion: around the door handles, the rear lights and on the bonnet. There were also some deep stone chips on the A-pillars and the front of the roof. The original plan was to paint only where it was needed: the front end, the door handles and around the rear lights. Once we inspected the car properly, we agreed every panel except the boot lid needed paint. After 14 years the front wings, bumper and bonnet had their fair share of stone chips, and the aluminium corrosion had appeared in the usual places around the car.

It took five long days in the body shop, working 10 to 14 hour days into and over the bank holiday weekend. The worst areas were stripped, cleaned, filled and primed, so the old stone chips wouldn't show through as pitting under the fresh paint. Then came several coats of base colour and three or four of clear, before the whole car was fully flatted and polished. The mirror caps didn't need painting, which made them a great way to check the colour match. It's spot on.

An enormous amount of effort, with spectacular results. It's great when your mate owns his own body shop! We had a great time doing it, and I learned so much about how the car is put together. Massive thanks to Jay for what turned out to be a behemoth of a task.

November 2022

Rear light condensation — permanent fix

Done ✓

Condensation in the rear clusters is a classic DB9 problem, and after a hand car wash the dash lit up with light errors. Aston's TSB (SB0286V2) just drills a small hole and dries the lamp out, so I devised a permanent fix instead. Both clusters came out and were split, cleaned and dried, then resealed with butyl. Each got a 50 mm grommeted access hole and three rechargeable silica gel sachets inside, so they stay dry and the gel can be renewed in minutes. The rear light gasket grommets were also replaced when the lights went back on. Not a drop of condensation since.

October 2022

Air filters, bulbs & DRLs

Done ✓

The air filters hadn't been done at the previous Aston Martin dealer service, and they definitely needed it. Getting to them means the undertray and arch liners off, so with that much effort already going in, I tackled the lights at the same time. With the bumper off, the lower grilles came out and were painted too.

The sidelight bulbs were swapped for Philips LEDs, and the side repeaters got the same treatment: Philips LEDs that look white when off but light orange. They look much better behind clear lenses, so the amber repeater lenses were swapped for clear ones while I was there.

I'd also seen DRL/indicator LED units elsewhere, available with the correct OEM plug. They turned out to be a bigger job than I'd expected! I machined better mounts from plastic rod, and built an interface box that picks up an ignition-on feed for the DRLs, so no original wiring was cut. As with everything I've done, the idea is to leave the OEM cabling undamaged. Everything connects through plugs, so if I ever want to take it fully back to standard, it's simply a case of reconnecting.

July 2022

Boot & door struts

Done ✓

The boot and door gas struts had lost their puff after 13 years, so both sets were replaced with genuine OEM Stabilus units supplied by Aston Martin Reading (boot 4G43-406A10-AC, doors 4G43-23504-AB).

The boot struts were very straightforward: prop the lid, flick out the retaining clips, and swap them. The door struts were more of a pain. Each door strut sits behind the front wheel-arch liner, so it's a case of wheel off, liner out, then working on the check-arm strut in a tight space with the door held wide open. Worth it, though: the doors and boot now open and stay open as they should.

June 2022

Main dealer oil service

Done ✓

An oil service at Aston Martin Reading came back with a full clean bill of health, plus a few advisories:

  • — Amber: boot struts and door struts. Fair enough: they were replaced the following month.
  • — Amber: brake discs and pads. I checked them myself and they were absolutely fine, so there was no way I was changing them at that point.

A good report overall, though you've got to give them something to feel good about on the report!

April 2022

Glass button upgrade

Done ✓

Later DB9s got genuine Aston Martin glass switchgear, and it looks far classier than the early plastic buttons. Once I'd seen the two side by side in photos, I had to make the change.

I sourced the glass buttons from a later car. Then I took the electronics out of the old, cheap-looking plastic buttons and moved them into the new glass housings. It's relatively straightforward, but a little fiddly. I did it in stages through the spring: PRND and the engine-start jewel first, then the Sport, parking and fog buttons to match.

What an upgrade to the interior. OEM AM glass buttons alongside the piano gloss waterfall: class.

Gloss black waterfall

Done ✓

The walnut trim was never my thing. I wanted piano gloss black for the centre-console waterfall instead. Rather than touch the original parts (a common theme with all my upgrades), I bought a second-hand waterfall to work on, and the originals are kept safe and untouched.

October 2021

Ambient air temperature sensor

Done ✓

The outside temperature on the dash was reading way off. That matters more than it sounds: the car also uses this sensor for battery charging, so with it reading far too hot, charging was affected. I replaced it with the OEM sensor from HWM.

It's an easy, straightforward job. The sensor sits behind the grille. Once the aluminium cover between the slam panel and the front of the engine is off, you literally pop the old sensor off, plug the new one in, and refit the cover.

October 2021

Mansory rear box

Removed ✗

It seemed like a great idea: a Mansory rear silencer to let the V12 be heard. In practice it turned the car into a droning, obnoxious mess. There was a terrible drone at every speed, and the refined, quality feel that makes a DB9 special was gone. Sadly, very chavtastic.

It came off again within weeks. Lesson learned. The proper answer turned out to be the secondary decat (2023), which gave the sound without the drone.
